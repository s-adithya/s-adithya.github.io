Teaching philosophy

My primary goal as an instructor is to make sure every student feels safe, welcome, and respected, and knows they belong in the mathematics classroom. Students arrive with very different experiences of mathematics, and some have had far more support and encouragement than others. I share the conviction behind Federico Ardila-Mantilla’s axioms for mathematics education: mathematical talent is distributed equally across groups of people, and every student deserves meaningful mathematical experiences and to be treated with dignity and respect.

My own interest in mathematics took time to develop. As a child I struggled with mathematics and physics, but I still vividly remember the experiments shown to us by a physics teacher who made a two-hour journey through Indian city traffic to teach our weekend classes. That teacher showed me how much an instructor can do by making room for curiosity and believing that students can understand. His example is why I volunteer with math circles and with science and mathematics camps for girls.

Students should do mathematics, not just watch it; they should do it together; and they should be cared for as whole people.

The three commitments that run through my teaching

Principles in practice

1

Doing mathematics, not just watching it

After introducing a concept and working an example, I give students time to try a related problem before I present a solution. I circulate, offering small hints and asking questions that help them find a next step. When a student is unsure or makes a mistake, my first concern is to help them feel at ease: an incomplete idea is still worth discussing, and I openly acknowledge my own mistakes.

2

Finding the right picture

A good picture often makes sense before a formal statement does. In MTH 314, I used geometric transformations of the plane so that students understood what eigenvalues and eigenvectors tell us about a linear transformation while they learned to compute them.

3

Learning together

Explaining an idea to a classmate is one of the best ways to understand it, and asking a classmate is often easier than asking the instructor. I build group work into class time in person, and I design online courses so that live sessions are spent working together.

4

Caring for the whole student

My students have lives and pressures outside my course. I offer flexible deadlines in difficult circumstances, space exams so students have time to prepare, and avoid scheduling an exam right after a long weekend or break. An exam is a fair measure of learning only when students have had a fair chance to prepare.

5

Mistakes as a reason to keep working

Standards-based grading in MTH 103A gave students further opportunities to show mastery of topics they first found difficult, so an unsuccessful attempt became a reason to keep working rather than a final verdict.

6

Not waiting for students to come to me

In an online course a struggling student can easily go unnoticed. In MTH 314 I wrote to students who were not doing well and met with them individually on Zoom.

Using AI to sharpen proof reading. In MTH 317H recitations I give students poorly written AI-generated proofs and ask them to find unjustified assumptions, missing steps, and errors that are easy to overlook when an explanation sounds convincing, and to say what the AI did well. The aim is for students to understand its possibilities and limits, and to gain the confidence to assess an argument for themselves, not to have AI do their homework.

Teaching across course contexts

MTH 103A: College Algebra I

Instructor of record · Spring 2025 · about 60 students

Taught in an active-learning classroom in the MSU Union, with round tables and screens throughout the room. Students worked in groups while two undergraduate learning assistants and I circulated, and I wrote live on an iPad projected to every table. The class met daily at 8 a.m. through a Michigan winter, so I offered a Zoom option for icy mornings and possible closures. I wrote my own lecture notes and worksheets and used standards-based grading.

MTH 124: Survey of Calculus

Instructor of record · Summer 2023 · fully online

Structured as a flipped classroom. Students watched videos I recorded before class, meeting new ideas at their own pace, so live sessions could be spent working problems together and consolidating the theory. The course ran on Piazza with engagement points, and students could optionally make a short “reel” introducing themselves to help them find classmates to work with.

MTH 314: Matrix Algebra with Applications

Instructor of record · Summer 2024 · fully online

I wrote lecture notes in LaTeX and also shared the handwritten notes produced live in each Zoom lecture, so students had both a polished reference and a record of how each argument unfolded. I recorded lecture videos, posted short check-in videos between meetings, and designed in-class Python assignments so students practiced the computational side of matrix algebra alongside the theory.

MTH 317H: Honors Linear Algebra

Recitation leader · Fall 2026

I teach the proof techniques from MTH 299 that students need but may not yet know, with worksheets that ask why a concept is useful or needed. Complete solutions are released twenty-four hours after each deadline so students can check their reasoning. Course materials →

I have also been instructor of record for MTH 234: Multivariable Calculus (Fall 2024), and a teaching assistant for courses including MTH 299 (Introduction to Proofs) and MTH 818 (Graduate Algebra I). See the full list on the Teaching page.
