Teaching Portfolio

My primary goal as an instructor is to make sure every student feels safe, welcome, and respected, and knows they belong in the mathematics classroom. Students arrive with very different experiences of mathematics, and some have had far more support and encouragement than others. I share the conviction behind Federico Ardila-Mantilla’s axioms for mathematics education that mathematical talent is distributed equally across groups of people, and every student deserves meaningful mathematical experiences and to be treated with dignity and respect.

Below are some aspects of my teaching philosophy, and how they show up in the courses I have taught. My full teaching statement (PDF) has more detail.

Teaching philosophy

In the classroom

  • Doing mathematics, not just watching it. After introducing a concept and working an example, I give students time to try a related problem before I present a solution. I walk around the room, offering small hints and asking questions that help them find a next step.
  • Mistakes are part of the work. When a student is unsure or makes a mistake, my first concern is to help them feel at ease. An incomplete idea is still worth discussing, and I openly acknowledge my own mistakes.
  • Finding the right picture. A good picture often makes sense before a formal statement does. In MTH 314 I used geometric transformations of the plane so that students understood what eigenvalues and eigenvectors tell us about a linear transformation while they learned to compute them.
  • Asking why, not just how. My MTH 317H worksheets ask why a concept is useful or needed, not only how to apply it.

Learning together

Explaining an idea to a classmate is one of the best ways to understand it, and asking a classmate is often easier than asking the instructor. In person, I build group work into class time. Online, where students rarely get to meet their peers, I design the course so that live sessions are spent working on problems together.

Caring for the whole student

  • Flexibility. My students have lives and pressures outside my course. When a student faces difficult circumstances, I offer flexible deadlines so they can complete the work rather than fall behind for reasons beyond their control.
  • A fair chance to prepare. I space exams so students have time to prepare, and I avoid scheduling one right after a long weekend or break.
  • Reaching out. In an online course a struggling student can easily go unnoticed. I write to students who are not doing well and meet with them individually.
  • Another chance to show mastery. With standards-based grading, an unsuccessful attempt becomes a reason to keep working rather than a final verdict.

Feedback

I check understanding with short exit tickets and low-stakes worksheets, and I release complete solutions after each deadline so students can check their own reasoning. I also keep listening to what students tell me about their learning, and adjust.

Technology, including AI

I use technology where it serves learning: live writing on an iPad projected to every table, recorded videos, Zoom options for bad-weather days, and Python assignments for the computational side of linear algebra. In MTH 317H recitations I give students poorly written AI-generated proofs and ask them to find unjustified assumptions, missing steps, and errors that are easy to overlook when an explanation sounds convincing, and to say what the AI did well. The aim is for students to understand both what AI can and cannot do, and to gain confidence in judging an argument for themselves. AI should not do their homework.

Teaching in different settings

MTH 103A: College Algebra I (instructor, Spring 2025). About sixty students in an active-learning classroom in the MSU Union, with round tables and screens throughout the room. Students worked in groups while two undergraduate learning assistants and I moved between tables. The class met daily at 8 a.m. through a Michigan winter, so I offered a Zoom option for icy mornings and possible closures. I wrote my own notes and worksheets and used standards-based grading.

MTH 124: Survey of Calculus (instructor, Summer 2023, online). I ran the course as a flipped classroom: students watched videos I recorded before class, so our live sessions could be spent working through problems and discussing solutions together. The course used Piazza for questions, and students could optionally record a short video introducing themselves to help them find classmates to work with.

MTH 314: Matrix Algebra with Applications (instructor, Summer 2024, online). I wrote lecture notes in LaTeX and also shared the handwritten notes from each Zoom lecture, so students had both a polished reference and a record of how each argument unfolded. I recorded lecture videos and short check-ins between meetings, and designed in-class Python assignments.

MTH 317H: Honors Linear Algebra (recitation leader, Fall 2026). I teach the proof techniques from MTH 299 that students need but may not yet know. The notes and worksheets are on the course page.

Resources for students

Learning and reading

  • 3Blue1Brown: visual explanations of mathematics, including a series on linear algebra
  • Keith Conrad’s expository papers: clear, self-contained notes on algebra and number theory
  • Terence Tao’s career advice: essays on studying and working in mathematics
  • Book of Proof by Richard Hammack, Linear Algebra Done Right by Sheldon Axler, and Linear Algebra Done Wrong by Sergei Treil, all free online
  • Springer textbooks: many can be downloaded free through MSU Libraries; sign in with your MSU NetID
  • OpenStax: free, peer-reviewed college textbooks
